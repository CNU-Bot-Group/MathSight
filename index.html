MathSight

A Benchmark Exploring Have Vision-Language Models Really Seen in University-Level Mathematical Reasoning?

Yuandong Wang Yao Cui Yuxin Zhao Zhen Yang* Yangfu Zhu Zhenzhou Shao
Capital Normal University
*Corresponding Author
📄 arXiv 💻 Code 📦 Dataset 📊 Statistics

Abstract

Recent advances in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have achieved impressive progress in multimodal mathematical reasoning. Yet, how much visual information truly contributes to reasoning remains unclear. Existing benchmarks report strong overall performance but seldom isolate the role of the image modality, leaving open whether VLMs genuinely leverage visual understanding or merely depend on linguistic priors.

To address this, we present MathSight, a university-level multimodal mathematical reasoning benchmark designed to disentangle and quantify the effect of visual input. Each problem includes multiple visual variants—original, hand-drawn, photo-captured—and a text-only condition for controlled comparison.

Experiments on state-of-the-art VLMs reveal a consistent trend: the contribution of visual information diminishes with increasing problem difficulty. Remarkably, Qwen3-VL without any image input surpasses both its multimodal variants and GPT-5, underscoring the need for benchmarks like MathSight to advance genuine vision-grounded reasoning in future models.

Why MathSight?

Recent VLMs achieve impressive scores on multimodal math benchmarks, but it remains unclear how much they truly benefit from visual input. Existing datasets usually provide only a single, clean diagram per problem and rarely isolate the contribution of the image modality. As a result, it is difficult to tell whether models genuinely perform vision-grounded reasoning or mainly rely on linguistic priors.

  • Visual robustness gap. Subtle changes in visual style (e.g., hand-drawn or photo-captured diagrams) can flip previously correct answers into wrong ones, revealing fragile visual understanding.:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}
  • Missing visual variants. Prior benchmarks typically offer only one image per problem and do not support controlled comparisons across different visual conditions.:contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}
  • Text-only confound. Many problems can be partially solved from text alone, making it hard to measure the true value of visual information.:contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

MathSight is designed to directly address these issues by providing controlled visual variants and a matched text-only setting for university-level mathematical reasoning.:contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

Dataset Overview

MathSight contains 2048 university-level math problems, including 1387 text-only items and 661 multimodal items paired with diagrams. Among the multimodal problems, 603 are at the graduate level, making MathSight one of the most challenging benchmarks in this space.:contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

Problems are drawn from six core mathematical domains: Calculus, Algebra, Analysis, Probability & Statistics, Discrete Mathematics, and Applied Mathematics, covering both foundational and advanced topics such as logic and topology.:contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

In addition, MathSight includes 30 proving problems (29 graduate-level), which cannot be evaluated by simple exact-match answers and are used to probe logical consistency in long-form reasoning.:contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

Example Problems

A few representative MathSight examples illustrating visually grounded mathematical reasoning.

(a) Function Plot Interpretation
Given a velocity–time plot of a particle, determine on which intervals the acceleration is negative and compute the total distance traveled over the first 10 seconds.
(b) Geometry with Diagram
A triangle with annotated side lengths and angles is shown. Using the diagram, find the length of segment AD and the area of the shaded region.
(c) Piecewise Graph Reasoning
From a graph of a piecewise-defined function, determine all points of discontinuity, classify each as removable or non-removable, and compute the corresponding one-sided limits.
(d) Probability Table
A contingency table for two events is shown in an image. Compute the conditional probability, odds ratio, and decide whether the events can be treated as independent.
(e) Algebra on the Blackboard
A photo of a blackboard shows a partially written system of equations. Recover the full system and solve for the unknown vector, explaining each algebraic transformation.
(f) Multi-step Word Problem
A word problem is paired with a supporting diagram (number line, bar chart, or schematic). Derive the correct symbolic formulation and compute the final numeric answer.
